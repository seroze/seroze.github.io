## The input illusion

The core claim: exposure feels like learning and isn't. Re-reading, highlighting,
watching a lecture at 1.5x — all of them raise familiarity while leaving retrieval
untouched. Familiarity is what gets measured in the moment; retrieval is what gets
measured later, when it matters.

## Retrieval is the actual mechanism

## Spacing, and why it feels bad

*Forgetting a little is the point — a review at the edge of recall does more than three
reviews while it's still fresh. Note the tradeoff: spaced practice performs worse during
the session and better a month later, which is exactly why nobody chooses it.*

## Interleaving vs. blocking

*Doing twenty problems of one type teaches you to execute a known method. Mixing types
teaches you to choose one — and choosing is the part that's actually hard in an exam,
an interview, or real work. Concrete example from CP: solving a whole DP set in a row
vs. a mixed set.*

## Learning is a tree, not a feed

**This is the one that matters most.** Knowledge has a shape: every node sits on top of
the ones below it, and you only get to stand on a node you've actually built. So the
question before starting any topic isn't "is this interesting" — it's "what is this
sitting on, and do I have it?" If the answer is no, that's the topic, not this one.

The test for a study session is simple and strict: did I move up a node? Not did I read
for two hours, not did I finish the chapter — is there one thing I can now build on that
I couldn't before. If I can't name it, the session didn't happen.

The common failure is jumping across nodes — picking up a fashionable topic four levels
above where I actually am, understanding the words and none of the substance, and
mistaking that for progress. It feels fast and it compounds into nothing, because there's
no node underneath to attach it to. Going back down and building the missing one feels
like a step backwards and is the only thing that works.

## Form your own opinion on it

**Repeating the teacher's version back is rote learning, however sophisticated it
sounds.** If the only thing I can produce is the explanation I was given, in the order I
was given it, I haven't understood anything — I've cached someone else's output. The
real check is whether I have a take of my own: why this approach and not the obvious
alternative, where it breaks down, which part of the standard explanation I think is
weak or badly motivated.

This isn't only about studying. Anything you take on someone else's authority is fine
right up until the situation shifts slightly off the one they described, and then you
have nothing to reason with. That's the cost, and it always arrives later, which is why
it's so easy to ignore now.
