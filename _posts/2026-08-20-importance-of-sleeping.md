I went looking for an answer to a small question — does sleeping produce vitamins? — and ended up somewhere more interesting. The short answer is no, but the long answer is that sleep decides how well your body can use the vitamins it already has, and that the two stages people care about, REM and deep sleep, are doing completely different jobs.

## Sleep doesn't make vitamins

Nothing is synthesized *because* you're asleep. The vitamins that your body does produce come from elsewhere: vitamin D from sunlight hitting your skin, vitamin K and biotin from gut bacteria working around the clock, niacin from tryptophan through a pathway that doesn't care what time it is.

What sleep does instead is run the machinery that spends those nutrients. Protein synthesis needs B vitamins. Tissue repair needs vitamin C and zinc. Bone remodeling depends on how vitamin D and calcium are being handled. All of that is scheduled around sleep, and absorption and utilization degrade when sleep does. So the honest framing is less "sleep makes vitamins" and more "sleep is when your body actually gets to use them."

## What REM is for

Adults spend roughly a fifth to a quarter of the night in REM, and the REM periods get longer toward morning — which is exactly the part you cut when you sleep six hours instead of eight.

The clearest thing REM does is consolidate memory, especially the procedural kind: skills, pattern recognition, the sense that a problem you were stuck on yesterday is somehow easier today. If you spent the day grinding competitive programming problems or reading papers, REM is where that gets filed.

It also seems to strip the emotional charge off memories while keeping the memory itself. That's the mechanism behind something feeling less awful the morning after, and it's why chronic REM deprivation tracks with anxiety and depression.

The creativity claim is related. During REM the brain links concepts that don't obviously belong together, which is a decent description of what an insight feels like from the inside.

And in infants REM can be half of total sleep, because it's building neural circuits, synapses, and sensory processing from scratch. Adults are running the maintenance version of the same process — strengthening the connections that got used, weakening the ones that didn't, so the network stays efficient instead of saturating.

## Deep sleep is doing the other half

REM gets the attention, but N3 deep sleep is where the body gets repaired. Most growth hormone release happens there. So does muscle recovery, immune function, and glymphatic clearance — the brain flushing out metabolic waste, which is a genuinely strange and recent thing to know about.

A normal adult night breaks down roughly as:

| Stage | Share of the night | What it's for |
|---|---|---|
| Light (N1 + N2) | 50–60% | transition, consolidation |
| Deep (N3) | 15–25% | physical repair, growth hormone, immune function, brain waste clearance |
| REM | 20–25% | learning, memory, creativity, emotional regulation, plasticity |

## So is more REM better?

No — the ratios are the point. Too little REM shows up as impaired learning and worse emotional regulation. Too little deep sleep shows up as a body that isn't recovering and an immune system that isn't keeping up. You want both, in something close to those proportions, which mostly means sleeping long enough that you don't truncate the REM-heavy back half of the night.

Sleep doesn't manufacture anything. It just decides how much of what you already have actually gets used.
